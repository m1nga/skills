# Copywriting Reference

Headline formulas, hook patterns, CTA patterns, and mini-rules for `prompt-craft` to inject into copy-related prompts.

> Brand names in examples are **fictional** ("Meridian" — invented market-data tool; "Openbook"/"Tally" — invented prediction-market venues). Example figures marked `[ILLUSTRATIVE]` must be replaced with real, verifiable numbers before use in actual copy.

---

## §1 Headline Formulas

Each formula has: the pattern, when to use it, an example, and a common mistake.

### 1. Number + Adjective + Noun + Promise

- **Pattern**: `[Number] [Adjective] [Noun] that [Promise]`
- **When**: listicles, how-tos, resource roundups
- **Example**: "7 Overlooked Signals That Moved Markets Last Week"
- **Mistake**: using "best" or "amazing" as the adjective (generic, overused)

### 2. How to [Desired Outcome] Without [Pain Point]

- **Pattern**: `How to [thing they want] without [thing they fear]`
- **When**: tutorials, guides, problem-solving content
- **Example**: "How to Catch Market Moves Without Scrolling Your Feed for 6 Hours"
- **Mistake**: vague outcome ("How to be successful") or vague pain ("without struggling")

### 3. The [Unexpected Noun] of [Topic]

- **Pattern**: `The [Surprising Noun] of [Familiar Topic]`
- **When**: contrarian takes, think pieces, brand content
- **Example**: "The Silence of the Fed (and What It Means for June Cuts)"
- **Mistake**: forced metaphor that doesn't actually illuminate

### 4. Why [Common Belief] Is Wrong

- **Pattern**: `Why [thing everyone believes] is [wrong/backwards/costing you]`
- **When**: contrarian content, myth-busting, strong-stance pieces
- **Example**: "Why Following Alpha Accounts Is Making You Slower"
- **Mistake**: straw-manning the common belief instead of engaging it honestly

### 5. [Do This], Not [That]

- **Pattern**: `[Better action], not [common action]`
- **When**: direct advice, tweet-length takes, brand positioning
- **Example**: "Trade the data. Not the vibes."
- **Mistake**: both sides too abstract ("Be smart, not dumb")

### 6. The Question Headline

- **Pattern**: `[Provocative question]?`
- **When**: debate starters, community engagement, polls. WARNING: some brand voices ban question openers as a marketing tell — check the user context before using
- **Example**: "Is the Fed priced in or priced out?"
- **Mistake**: question with an obvious answer ("Want to make better trades?")

### 7. [Specific Number] + [Timeframe]

- **Pattern**: `[Exact number] [thing] in [timeframe]`
- **When**: proof, receipts, data-driven content
- **Example**: "4 signals in 90 minutes. All hit." `[ILLUSTRATIVE]`
- **Mistake**: rounded/vague numbers (specificity = credibility)

### 8. The Before/After

- **Pattern**: `[Before state]. [After state].`
- **When**: transformation stories, product demos, testimonials
- **Example**: "Tuesday: scrolling the feed for an hour. Wednesday: Meridian alert at 6:47am."
- **Mistake**: before too abstract, after too aspirational (keep both concrete)

### 9. The Warning

- **Pattern**: `[Warning]: [consequence of inaction]`
- **When**: urgency content, limited-time, important updates
- **Example**: "This signal hit at 2:14pm. By 2:30pm it was already priced in."
- **Mistake**: fake urgency / crying wolf

### 10. The "I [Past Tense]" Personal Proof

- **Pattern**: `I [did specific thing] and [specific result]`
- **When**: personal brand content, testimonials, case studies. For brand accounts with an observer voice: variant → "[Person/Signal] [did thing] and [result]"
- **Example**: "Caught the FOMC signal 12 minutes early. Here's the receipt."
- **Mistake**: humble-bragging or vague results

### 11. The Curiosity Gap

- **Pattern**: `[Incomplete statement that demands completion]`
- **When**: email subject lines, tweet hooks, video titles
- **Example**: "Openbook 71¢. Tally 65¢. Same market."
- **Mistake**: gap so wide it feels clickbait; gap so narrow there's no intrigue

### 12. The Contrast

- **Pattern**: `[Thing A] vs [Thing B]: [judgment]`
- **When**: comparisons, opinion pieces, product positioning
- **Example**: "71 sources in 2 minutes vs. 2 hours of scrolling. Pick." `[ILLUSTRATIVE]`
- **Mistake**: unfair comparison that insults the audience's current behavior

### 13. The Imperative

- **Pattern**: `[Strong verb] + [specific object]`
- **When**: CTAs, product launches, action-oriented content
- **Example**: "Watch this market." / "Pull up the Fed speakers list."
- **Mistake**: generic imperative ("Discover more")

### 14. The Social Proof Headline

- **Pattern**: `[Number] [people] [action] [timeframe]`
- **When**: landing pages, ads, conversion-focused content
- **Example**: "1,200 traders checking signals before the bell. Every morning." `[ILLUSTRATIVE — replace with real, verifiable numbers]`
- **Mistake**: inflated numbers without receipts

### 15. The "Most People... But..."

- **Pattern**: `Most [people] [common action]. [Contrarian alternative].`
- **When**: positioning, contrarian takes, differentiation
- **Example**: "Most traders check the feed after the move. Some see it 12 minutes before."
- **Mistake**: insulting the "most people" group (your audience is in that group until they convert)

### 16. The Timestamp

- **Pattern**: `[Time]: [event]. [Later time]: [consequence].`
- **When**: receipt-style proof, signal catches, market moves
- **Example**: "2:14pm: Meridian flags Fed signal. 2:26pm: market moves 9¢."
- **Mistake**: timestamps without enough context to understand significance

## §2 Hook Patterns

For opening lines of any content (tweets, videos, emails, ads). Each hook has: the mechanism, best use, and an example.

### Hook 1: Pattern Interrupt

- **Mechanism**: say something that doesn't fit the expected category
- **Best use**: social media, video openings
- **Example**: "Stop scrolling your feed."
- **Why it works**: breaks autopilot, forces attention

### Hook 2: Shocking Stat

- **Mechanism**: lead with a number that surprises
- **Best use**: data-driven content, tweets, video intros
- **Example**: "71 sources. 2 minutes. 900 markets." `[ILLUSTRATIVE]`
- **Why it works**: specificity creates credibility; surprising scale creates intrigue

### Hook 3: Contrarian Claim

- **Mechanism**: state the opposite of what the audience expects
- **Best use**: opinion content, threads, video essays
- **Example**: "More information is making you a worse trader."
- **Why it works**: creates cognitive dissonance that demands resolution

### Hook 4: The Receipt

- **Mechanism**: show proof before making any claim
- **Best use**: X/Twitter, trading content, product demos
- **Example**: "[screenshot of signal catch with timestamp]"
- **Why it works**: proof-first bypasses skepticism; the claim becomes self-evident

### Hook 5: In Medias Res

- **Mechanism**: start in the middle of the action
- **Best use**: stories, case studies, video scripts
- **Example**: "2:14pm. FOMC signal fires. I'm already in the trade."
- **Why it works**: drops the reader into momentum; no ramp-up needed

### Hook 6: The Question (use carefully)

- **Mechanism**: ask something the audience genuinely wonders about
- **Best use**: community engagement, polls, forum posts. WARNING: some brand voices ban question openers as a marketing tell — check the user context
- **Example**: "What moved the Fed futures overnight?"
- **Why it works**: activates curiosity. BUT only works if the question is genuinely interesting, not rhetorical marketing.

### Hook 7: Direct Address

- **Mechanism**: call out a specific person type
- **Best use**: ads, emails, targeted content
- **Example**: "Traders spending 3+ hours a day scrolling for signals:"
- **Why it works**: self-selection — the right audience stops, everyone else scrolls

### Hook 8: The Gap

- **Mechanism**: show two things that should match but don't
- **Best use**: arbitrage content, market analysis, contrarian takes
- **Example**: "Openbook 71¢. Tally 65¢. Same question."
- **Why it works**: the gap IS the content; the reader immediately wants to know why

### Hook 9: The Before/After Flash

- **Mechanism**: compressed transformation in 2 beats
- **Best use**: product demos, testimonials, ads
- **Example**: "Before: 2 hours scrolling. After: 2-minute scan."
- **Why it works**: transformation is the most powerful narrative structure

### Hook 10: The Confession

- **Mechanism**: admit something vulnerable or counterintuitive
- **Best use**: personal brand content, thought leadership, threads
- **Example**: "I was wrong about the Fed cut thesis. Here's what I missed."
- **Why it works**: vulnerability creates trust; admission of error signals credibility

### Hook 11: The List Tease

- **Mechanism**: promise a specific number of valuable items
- **Best use**: threads, carousels, listicles, emails
- **Example**: "5 markets mispriced right now. Thread ↓"
- **Why it works**: numbered promise creates expectation and commitment to read

### Hook 12: The Cold Open

- **Mechanism**: start with dialogue, sound, or action — no setup
- **Best use**: video scripts, podcasts, narrative content
- **Example**: *[screen recording of signal alert popping up, market chart moving]*
- **Why it works**: sensory engagement before cognitive engagement

## §3 CTA Patterns

### CTA 1: First-Person Action

- **Pattern**: "Get my [thing]" / "Start my [thing]"
- **When**: landing pages, ads, email buttons
- **Example**: "Get the Chrome extension"
- **Note**: first-person ("my") often outperforms second-person ("your") in English for conversion. Test in target market.

### CTA 2: Specific Next Step

- **Pattern**: "[Exact action] → [exact outcome]"
- **When**: any CTA position
- **Example**: "Install → see your first signal in 2 minutes"
- **Note**: specificity of outcome is what converts, not the verb

### CTA 3: Low-Friction Trial

- **Pattern**: "Try [thing] free — [qualifier that reduces risk]"
- **When**: product trials, freemium
- **Example**: "Free. No rate limits. Just install."
- **Note**: every additional word of friction ("sign up", "create account", "enter email") reduces conversion

### CTA 4: Social Proof CTA

- **Pattern**: "Join [number] [people] who [action]"
- **When**: community-building, newsletter signups
- **Example**: "Join 1,200 traders getting signals before the bell" `[ILLUSTRATIVE — number must be real]`
- **Note**: number must be real and specific (not "thousands")

### CTA 5: Urgency CTA

- **Pattern**: "[Action] before [deadline/scarcity]"
- **When**: limited offers, launches, events
- **Example**: "Beta access closes Friday"
- **Note**: fake urgency destroys trust permanently. Only use real deadlines.

### CTA 6: Continuation CTA

- **Pattern**: "[Read/Watch/Listen] [next piece]"
- **When**: content series, threads, funnels
- **Example**: "Thread continues ↓" / "Part 2 tomorrow"
- **Note**: builds habit, not just one-time action

### CTA 7: Reply/Engage CTA

- **Pattern**: "[Question that invites response]"
- **When**: X/Twitter, community posts, engagement-driving
- **Example**: "What market are you watching this week?"
- **Note**: strong-performing X CTA type — replies boost algorithmic distribution

### CTA 8: Bookmark/Save CTA

- **Pattern**: "Save this [for when you need it]"
- **When**: reference content, resource posts, how-tos
- **Example**: "Bookmark this. You'll need it on FOMC day."
- **Note**: saves/bookmarks are increasingly weighted by algorithms (X, IG)

## §4 Mini-Rules

Sentence-level copywriting rules. These are injected into prompts as constraints.

### Rule 1: One idea per sentence

If a sentence has "and" connecting two different ideas, split it. Exception: parallel structure for rhythm ("We scan. We match. We deliver.").

### Rule 2: Cut the first paragraph

First drafts almost always throat-clear. The real opening is usually paragraph 2. Cut paragraph 1 and see if the piece improves (it usually does).

### Rule 3: Specific > general, always

"12 minutes" > "fast". "71 sources" > "many sources". "2:14pm" > "earlier today". Every number, name, and timestamp makes copy more credible — provided the numbers are real.

### Rule 4: Active voice by default

"Meridian flags the signal" > "The signal is flagged by Meridian". Passive is only acceptable when the receiver of the action is more important than the actor.

### Rule 5: Read it aloud

If you can't say it naturally in one breath, it's too long. If you stumble, the reader will stumble. Rewrite until it flows.

### Rule 6: Delete adjectives first

When cutting for length, adjectives go first. They're almost always the lowest-information-density words in a sentence. "Our innovative, cutting-edge, powerful platform" → "Meridian."

### Rule 7: The "so what" test

After every claim, ask "so what?" If you can't answer in terms of the reader's life, the claim is a feature, not a benefit. Keep asking "so what?" until you hit an emotional payoff.

### Rule 8: Parallel structure for lists

Every item in a list should follow the same grammatical pattern. Bad: "We offer speed, reliability, and we also help you save money." Good: "Speed. Reliability. Savings."

### Rule 9: End on the strongest word

The last word of a sentence carries the most weight. "Meridian gives you the signal first" > "First, Meridian gives you the signal." Rearrange sentences so the power word lands last.

### Rule 10: Ban weasel words

"Very", "really", "quite", "somewhat", "fairly", "rather", "pretty" (as modifier) — all weaken the words they modify. "Very fast" < "fast" < "12 minutes early". Delete the modifier or find a stronger word.

## How `prompt-craft` uses this file

1. **Headline prompts**: inject 3-4 relevant formulas from §1 as scaffold options.
2. **Hook writing**: inject relevant hook patterns from §2 based on platform and format.
3. **CTA specification**: inject matching CTA pattern(s) from §3.
4. **All copy prompts**: inject relevant mini-rules from §4 as constraints.
5. **In rationale**: cite specific patterns: "copywriting.md §headline-7 — timestamp format for receipt-style proof."
6. **Cross-reference**: check patterns against the user context's §Brand Voice — some patterns may be banned for the user's brand (e.g., question openers).
