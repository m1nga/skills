# Brand Voice Reference

Reference for `prompt-craft` when drafting brand-voice prompts, auditing tone, or embedding voice constraints into any content prompt.

> Brand names and numbers in the examples below are **fictional/illustrative** ("Meridian" is an invented market-data tool). The sentence patterns are the teaching payload — swap in your own brand's facts from the user context.

---

## §1 The 12 Jungian Archetypes

Each archetype has: core desire, best audience fit, voice traits, do/don't, and brand examples.

### 1. The Innocent

- **Core desire**: Safety, happiness, simplicity
- **Best audience**: families, health-conscious, nostalgia-seekers
- **Voice traits**: optimistic, honest, simple, warm, wholesome
- **DO**: use plain language, evoke nostalgia, promise simplicity
- **DON'T**: be cynical, use complex jargon, acknowledge dark realities too heavily
- **Examples**: Coca-Cola (classic era), Dove, Whole Foods

### 2. The Explorer

- **Core desire**: Freedom, discovery, self-fulfillment through experience
- **Best audience**: adventurers, outdoor enthusiasts, independence-seekers
- **Voice traits**: bold, adventurous, independent, restless, authentic
- **DO**: evoke journey and discovery, use sensory language, celebrate independence
- **DON'T**: use corporate language, imply conformity, over-structure the narrative
- **Examples**: Patagonia, Jeep, The North Face, REI

### 3. The Sage

- **Core desire**: Truth, knowledge, understanding
- **Best audience**: intellectuals, researchers, information-seekers, B2B
- **Voice traits**: authoritative, analytical, informed, measured, credible
- **DO**: cite evidence, use precise language, respect intelligence, teach
- **DON'T**: dumb down, use emotional manipulation, make unsubstantiated claims
- **Examples**: Google, The Economist, Harvard Business Review, McKinsey

### 4. The Hero

- **Core desire**: Mastery, proving worth through courageous action
- **Best audience**: achievers, athletes, competitive professionals
- **Voice traits**: bold, determined, confident, empowering, direct
- **DO**: celebrate achievement, issue challenges, show transformation through effort
- **DON'T**: be arrogant, dismiss struggle, promise easy wins
- **Examples**: Nike, Under Armour, FedEx, BMW (performance line)

### 5. The Outlaw / Rebel

- **Core desire**: Revolution, liberation, breaking conventions
- **Best audience**: counterculture, disruptors, rule-breakers, younger demographics
- **Voice traits**: provocative, disruptive, unapologetic, raw, authentic
- **DO**: challenge status quo, use edgy language, show destruction of old ways
- **DON'T**: be gratuitously offensive, lose substance behind shock value, alienate without purpose
- **Examples**: Harley-Davidson, Diesel, Virgin, early Apple

### 6. The Magician

- **Core desire**: Transformation, making dreams reality
- **Best audience**: visionaries, experience-seekers, transformation-seekers
- **Voice traits**: visionary, inspiring, mysterious, transformative, charismatic
- **DO**: promise transformation, use vivid imagery, create "before/after" narratives
- **DON'T**: over-promise, be vague about the mechanism, use too much mysticism for practical products
- **Examples**: Apple (modern), Disney, Tesla, Dyson

### 7. The Regular Guy / Everyperson

- **Core desire**: Belonging, connection, fitting in
- **Best audience**: mainstream, value-seekers, community-oriented
- **Voice traits**: friendly, relatable, unpretentious, down-to-earth, practical
- **DO**: use everyday language, show real people, emphasize affordability and reliability
- **DON'T**: be elitist, use aspiration as primary driver, look down on the audience
- **Examples**: IKEA, Target, Budweiser, Gap

### 8. The Lover

- **Core desire**: Intimacy, sensory experience, passion
- **Best audience**: aesthetes, luxury buyers, experience-seekers, couples
- **Voice traits**: sensual, elegant, passionate, indulgent, warm
- **DO**: engage senses, use rich imagery, celebrate beauty and pleasure
- **DON'T**: be crude, focus on function over form, use clinical language
- **Examples**: Chanel, Victoria's Secret, Godiva, Häagen-Dazs

### 9. The Jester

- **Core desire**: Joy, living in the moment, lightness
- **Best audience**: young adults, entertainment-seekers, stressed professionals seeking relief
- **Voice traits**: humorous, irreverent, playful, witty, surprising
- **DO**: use humor, break fourth wall, be self-aware, entertain first
- **DON'T**: be mean-spirited, sacrifice clarity for comedy, be funny about serious pain points
- **Examples**: Old Spice, M&M's, Dollar Shave Club, Wendy's (Twitter)

### 10. The Caregiver

- **Core desire**: Protecting and caring for others
- **Best audience**: parents, caregivers, health-conscious, service workers
- **Voice traits**: nurturing, reassuring, generous, compassionate, supportive
- **DO**: show empathy, use "we" language, demonstrate protection and care
- **DON'T**: be condescending, guilt-trip, imply weakness in the audience
- **Examples**: Johnson & Johnson, Volvo, TOMS, Campbell's

### 11. The Ruler

- **Core desire**: Control, stability, creating order from chaos
- **Best audience**: executives, luxury buyers, status-conscious, leaders
- **Voice traits**: commanding, refined, authoritative, exclusive, premium
- **DO**: convey status, use premium language, promise quality and control
- **DON'T**: be accessible/casual, use discount language, over-explain
- **Examples**: Rolex, Mercedes-Benz, American Express (Black), Bloomberg Terminal

### 12. The Creator

- **Core desire**: Innovation, self-expression, bringing vision to life
- **Best audience**: artists, designers, entrepreneurs, makers
- **Voice traits**: imaginative, expressive, unconventional, inspiring, original
- **DO**: celebrate originality, show process, inspire creation, use unique visual language
- **DON'T**: follow trends blindly, use generic creative language, copy competitors
- **Examples**: Adobe, Lego, Moleskine, Pinterest

## §2 Picking an Archetype

### The decision framework

1. **Start with audience, not brand.** What archetype does your target audience IDENTIFY with? Match that, or pick the complementary guide archetype.
2. **Check for dissonance.** Archetype must align with: product category, price point, distribution channel, and audience worldview. Luxury + Jester = risky. Budget + Ruler = laughable.
3. **One primary, one secondary.** Most strong brands blend two archetypes: a primary (80%) and a secondary flavor (20%). Example: Apple = Magician (primary) + Creator (secondary).
4. **Test against competitors.** If your top 3 competitors are all Heroes, consider an adjacent archetype. Differentiation > conformity.
5. **Validate with real content.** Write 5 tweets in the archetype. Do they feel natural for the brand? If forced, wrong archetype.

### Common archetype mistakes

- Picking aspirationally (what you wish the brand were) instead of authentically (what the brand actually is)
- Switching archetypes across platforms (confuses brand recognition)
- Choosing Outlaw/Rebel because it sounds cool without the cultural credibility to back it
- Picking Sage when the audience wants Hero (common B2B mistake — they want empowerment, not education)

## §3 The Tone Ladder — 4 Axes

Each axis is a spectrum, not a binary. Rate your brand on each axis (1-5) to create a tone fingerprint.

### Axis 1: Formality

| 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Slang, fragments, emoji | Casual contractions, short sentences | Professional but approachable | Polished, no contractions | Academic, formal structure |

**Example at each level**:
- 1: "lol this market is cooked"
- 2: "Yeah, this market's not pricing that in."
- 3: "The current market pricing doesn't reflect the latest Fed commentary."
- 4: "Current market valuations do not adequately account for recent Federal Reserve statements."
- 5: "An analysis of current market valuations reveals a systematic underpricing of monetary policy risk factors."

### Axis 2: Energy

| 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Deadpan, understated | Calm, measured | Balanced warmth | Enthusiastic, forward-leaning | High-energy, urgent, exclamatory |

**Example at each level**:
- 1: "Meridian caught this 12 minutes early."
- 2: "Our signals flagged this move 12 minutes before the broader feed."
- 3: "We spotted this signal 12 minutes ahead — here's what it looked like."
- 4: "We caught this 12 minutes before anyone else — and the data was crystal clear."
- 5: "12 MINUTES EARLY. The signal was screaming. Here's the receipt!"

### Axis 3: Humor

| 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dead serious, no levity | Dry wit, irony | Occasional humor, light touch | Frequently funny, playful | Comedy-first, entertainment brand |

**Example at each level**:
- 1: "Signal detected at 14:23 EST. Market moved at 14:35."
- 2: "someone is front-running something" *(dry observation)*
- 3: "The market hasn't priced this in. Shocking, we know."
- 4: "Fed speakers: 'rates aren't moving.' Market: 67¢ on June cuts. Math ain't mathing."
- 5: "POV: you're a prediction-market degen watching the Fed presser and your thesis just got validated in real-time 📈"

### Axis 4: Warmth

| 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cold, impersonal, pure data | Detached but fair | Neutral, balanced | Empathetic, supportive | Intimate, personal, caring |

**Example at each level**:
- 1: "71 sources. 2 min scan. 900 markets."
- 2: "Meridian scans 71 sources every 2 minutes across 900 markets."
- 3: "We scan 71 sources every 2 minutes so you can focus on the markets that matter to you."
- 4: "We know the information game is exhausting. That's why Meridian scans 71 sources every 2 minutes — so you can trade, not scroll."
- 5: "You've been scrolling for hours looking for the one signal that matters. We get it. Let Meridian do the scrolling."

### Example tone fingerprint

**Must be defined per-brand in the user context** — this is what a filled-in fingerprint looks like (for the fictional Meridian, a deadpan data-first tool):

- Formality: **2** (casual contractions, short sentences, fragments OK)
- Energy: **1-2** (deadpan to calm — never exclamatory)
- Humor: **2** (dry wit, irony — never comedy-first)
- Warmth: **1-2** (cold to detached — data-first, observer voice)

If the user context has no fingerprint yet, prompt-craft should propose one from the persona's register and label it `[ASSUMPTION]`.

## §4 Do/Don't Patterns — Operational Voice Rules

These translate archetype + tone ladder into sentence-level decisions.

### Pattern: Opening lines

- **DO**: start mid-thought, as if conversation is already happening
- **DON'T**: throat-clear ("In today's world...", "Have you ever...", "Let me tell you about...")
- **Test**: cover the first sentence. Does the second sentence still make sense? If yes, delete the first.

### Pattern: Proof and claims

- **DO**: receipt first, claim second. Show the data, then name what it means.
- **DON'T**: claim first, then scramble for proof. "Best in class" followed by a feature list = backwards.
- **Test**: can you delete the claim and let the proof speak? If yes, delete the claim.

### Pattern: Sentence length

- **DO**: vary. Short sentences for impact. Medium for explanation. Alternate.
- **DON'T**: every sentence same length (monotonous) or every sentence short (breathless) or every sentence long (exhausting).
- **Test**: read aloud. If you can't breathe naturally, rewrite.

### Pattern: Technical terminology

- **DO**: use terms your audience uses daily without explanation.
- **DON'T**: explain basics. "Prediction markets, which are..." — if they're reading your content, they know.
- **Test**: would your target persona stop and define this term to a colleague? No → don't define it.

### Pattern: CTA language

- **DO**: specific action + specific outcome. "Get the extension → see signals before the feed."
- **DON'T**: vague ("Learn more", "Get started", "Discover").
- **Test**: could this CTA be for any product? If yes, too generic.

### Pattern: Competitor mentions

- **DO**: show contrast through data, not name-calling. "X takes 15 min. This takes 2."
- **DON'T**: name competitors in organic content unless quoting them.
- **Exception**: the user context may override for specific competitive contexts.

## §5 Common Brand Voice Failures

### Failure 1: Tone words without operational rules

- **Symptom**: brand guide says "friendly, professional, trustworthy" — copywriter produces generic content that could be anyone.
- **Fix**: every tone word needs 2 DO examples and 2 DON'T examples at sentence level.

### Failure 2: Voice varies by platform without a thread

- **Symptom**: TikTok is playful, LinkedIn is corporate, X is edgy — brand feels like 3 different companies.
- **Fix**: one archetype, one tone fingerprint. Platform adaptation = volume dial, not personality swap.

### Failure 3: Voice is a costume, not a personality

- **Symptom**: voice guide exists but content doesn't sound like it. The guide was aspirational, not derived from real content that worked.
- **Fix**: build voice from existing high-performing content, not from a brand workshop. What already works IS the voice.

### Failure 4: Mistaking energy for voice

- **Symptom**: brand guide is all about energy level ("dynamic", "bold", "exciting") but says nothing about what the brand actually sounds like.
- **Fix**: energy is one axis (Axis 2 above). Voice is all four axes plus archetype plus do/don't patterns.

### Failure 5: Voice for the CEO, not for the customer

- **Symptom**: brand sounds how the founder talks in board meetings. Audience doesn't care about board meetings.
- **Fix**: voice must match the audience's register. Sage archetype for a Jester audience = alienation.

### Failure 6: No banned list

- **Symptom**: everything is theoretically in-voice because nothing is explicitly out-of-voice.
- **Fix**: a banned list is more useful than a style guide. 20 banned phrases do more work than 20 pages of guidelines.

## How `prompt-craft` uses this file

1. When drafting brand-voice prompts or auditing tone: pull archetype framework + tone ladder.
2. When embedding voice into content prompts: pull do/don't patterns.
3. In rationale: cite specific axis scores and patterns: "brand-voice.md §tone-ladder — formality 2, energy 1."
4. Cross-reference with the user context's §Brand Voice for the user's brand-specific rules.
5. Flag archetype mismatches: if a prompt asks for content that conflicts with the established archetype, warn.
