# Marketing Frameworks

Reference for `prompt-craft` drafting (Step L3 / D3). These are **skeletons**, not templates. `prompt-craft` should use them as starting scaffolds and explicitly adapt in the rationale — never apply blindly.

Each entry has: when to use, the structure, a concrete example, and common mistakes.

---

## §1 AIDA — Attention, Interest, Desire, Action

**When to use**: short-form direct-response (ads, email subject lines, landing page hero). Classic, works best when you need a complete persuasion arc in a tight space.

**Structure**:
1. **Attention** — stop the scroll. Hook, statistic, contrarian claim, visual surprise.
2. **Interest** — make them care. Introduce a problem they recognize as theirs.
3. **Desire** — show the outcome they want. Emotional payoff of the transformation.
4. **Action** — tell them exactly what to do next. One step, specific.

**Example (TikTok beauty ad script)**:
- A: "Stop using toner. You're wasting your skincare money."
- I: "Most toners are just water + alcohol + fragrance. They're drying out the very skin you're trying to heal."
- D: "Here's what to use instead — a pH-balancing essence that replaces toner AND serum in one step. My skin hasn't been this calm in two years."
- A: "Link in bio. Bundle's 30% off until Friday."

**Common mistakes**:
- Spending too long in A and I, leaving no room for D and A. Short-form needs 20/30/30/20 split, not 50/30/15/5.
- Using D as a feature list instead of an outcome. Bad: "contains niacinamide". Good: "the redness that's been on my cheeks for 10 years is gone".
- Using A as the CTA. "Buy now" is not Attention. "Buy now" is Action. Attention must earn the right to reach Action.

## §2 PAS — Problem, Agitate, Solve

**When to use**: pain-driven purchases (problem-aware audiences), email body copy, long-form ad copy, sales pages. Weaker for aspirational/luxury where pain framing breaks the vibe.

**Structure**:
1. **Problem** — name the pain they're living with.
2. **Agitate** — make it feel urgent. Show the compounding cost of not solving it.
3. **Solve** — present your solution as the specific answer to the agitated pain.

**Example (email for productivity SaaS)**:
- P: "Your inbox has 4,200 unread messages."
- A: "Every morning you spend 40 minutes deciding what to open. By noon you've forgotten what you were supposed to focus on. By Friday you've done 200 hours of 'work' and shipped nothing that matters."
- S: "<Product> surfaces the 5 emails you actually need to read. The other 4,195 get auto-sorted. Here's how 2,000 other PMs took back their mornings →"

**Common mistakes**:
- Agitate is tepid. "It can be annoying" ≠ agitation. The A must sting.
- Solve arrives without a visible bridge from the pain. "Try <product>" without saying how <product> maps to the specific pain just named.
- PAS on an audience that isn't problem-aware. If they don't know they have the pain, PAS feels aggressive and weird.

## §3 BAB — Before, After, Bridge

**When to use**: transformation stories, testimonials, case studies, before/after visual ads. Great for health, fitness, skincare, finance turnaround, productivity.

**Structure**:
1. **Before** — the painful status quo, vividly.
2. **After** — the transformed state, equally vivid.
3. **Bridge** — the specific thing that got them from Before to After.

**Example (fitness app)**:
- B: "Six months ago Sarah couldn't climb the stairs to her apartment without stopping for breath. She avoided social events because she didn't want to be photographed."
- A: "Today she ran her first 5K. Her husband says he hasn't seen her smile like this in years."
- Bridge: "The change started with one thing: 12 minutes a day, following the Couch-to-5K program inside <app>."

**Common mistakes**:
- Before is too abstract ("she was unhappy") — should be sensory and specific.
- After is a feature list instead of an outcome ("she used our app daily") — wrong thing in the After slot.
- Bridge is vague ("she made a change") — the Bridge must be the concrete, repeatable mechanism the audience can imagine doing themselves.

## §4 FAB — Features, Advantages, Benefits

**When to use**: product marketing, comparison pages, sales enablement, B2B decks. Helps writers translate feature-dumps into benefit framing.

**Structure** (for each feature):
1. **Feature** — the objective, verifiable attribute.
2. **Advantage** — what the feature lets the product do that competitors can't (or can't as well).
3. **Benefit** — what the advantage means for the user's life/work.

**Example (B2B analytics tool)**:
- F: "Event tracking runs client-side with 2ms latency."
- A: "You see user behavior in real time, not delayed by 10 minutes like most tools."
- B: "When something breaks at 3pm on launch day, your team fixes it by 3:02 — not 3:15 after losing 10,000 users."

**Common mistakes**:
- Skipping Advantage and jumping straight to Benefit. Readers don't buy into "you'll save time" without knowing what specifically makes this tool faster.
- Stacking FAB for every feature. Pick the 2-3 features that actually differentiate. Everything else can be a spec sheet at the bottom.
- Benefit that's just a restated advantage. "You see it in real time → which means you get real-time visibility." Circular. Benefit must be an outcome in the user's world, not a restatement.

## §5 StoryBrand (Donald Miller) — 7-part brand story

**When to use**: brand messaging, website copy, brand story videos, investor decks, pitch decks. Also great as a structural audit of existing brand copy.

**Structure** (the hero is the customer, not the brand):
1. **A character** — the customer, with a clear want.
2. **Has a problem** — external (surface), internal (emotional), philosophical (why it's unjust).
3. **Meets a guide** — the brand, with authority and empathy.
4. **Who gives them a plan** — concrete 3-step path.
5. **And calls them to action** — direct CTA and transitional CTA.
6. **That helps them avoid failure** — what's at stake if they don't act.
7. **And ends in success** — the transformed life.

**Common mistakes**:
- Making the brand the hero. The brand is the guide, always.
- Skipping internal and philosophical problems. External problem alone is weak.
- Plan is vague. "Work with us" is not a plan. 3 concrete steps.
- No failure stakes. No cost to inaction = no reason to act.

## §6 Jobs To Be Done (JTBD)

**When to use**: positioning, product narrative, audience research, category creation.

**Structure**: When I [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome].

**Common mistakes**:
- Writing the job as a feature. "I want to use a meal kit" is not a job.
- Treating demographics as the job. Jobs are circumstantial and motivational.
- Only one job per product. Most products fulfill 2-4 jobs.

---

## §7 4Ps — Product, Price, Place, Promotion

**When to use**: marketing mix audits, go-to-market planning. Less useful for direct copywriting.

**Common mistakes**:
- Treating 4Ps as a checklist not an integrated system. Premium price + mass-market place = dissonance.
- Missing the "who" dimension. Always combine with JTBD or persona.

---

## §8 AARRR — Pirate Metrics

**When to use**: growth marketing, funnel audits, campaign strategy.

Structure: Acquisition → Activation → Retention → Referral → Revenue

**Common mistakes**:
- Optimizing Acquisition without checking Activation. Cheap installs that never activate are worthless.
- Defining Activation vaguely. Must be a concrete, measurable first-value event.

---

## §9 Hook-Story-Offer (Russell Brunson)

**When to use**: direct-response ads, sales pages, webinars. Simpler than AIDA, arguably more robust for DR at scale.

Structure: Hook (stop scroll) → Story (why believe) → Offer (what they get, why now)

**Common mistakes**:
- Hook that's a question to nobody. "Want to 10x?" is noise.
- Story is the brand's origin instead of the customer's pivot.
- Offer without scarcity/urgency.

---

## §10 3-Act Structure (for video ads)

**When to use**: video ads 15-90 seconds, any video with a complete arc under 2 minutes.

Structure:
1. **Act 1 (0-25%)** — setup + hook in first 1-3 seconds
2. **Act 2 (25-75%)** — confrontation, problem shown, obstacle, realization
3. **Act 3 (75-100%)** — resolution, transformation, CTA

**Common mistakes**:
- Act 1 takes too long. Hook must be in first 1-3 seconds.
- Act 2 has no obstacle. Without "but then..." the story is flat.
- Act 3 is all CTA and no resolution. Show transformation before asking for action.

1. In rationale: "Scaffolded around <framework §N>, adapted where <reason>."
